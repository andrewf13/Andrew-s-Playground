Science notes

The Long Fall is a live gravitational simulation run in your browser, edited like a film. Everything you see is computed — no stock footage, no recorded music.

The forecast

  • Andromeda (M31) is about 2.5 million light-years away and approaching the Milky Way at roughly 110 km/s. Hubble proper-motion measurements in 2012 (van der Marel et al.) predicted a first close pass in about 4 billion years and a single merged galaxy about 6 billion years from now. NASA
  • NASA’s 2012 sequence of the future night sky inspired chapter II. ESA/Hubble
  • In June 2025 Sawala et al. (Nature Astronomy), using Hubble and Gaia data and including M33 and the Large Magellanic Cloud, found only about a 50% chance of a merger in the next 10 billion years. Durham University · National Geographic
  • In April 2026 Wu et al. (The Astrophysical Journal Letters) re-ran the analysis with updated Gaia proper motions and found about a 90% merger probability in their fiducial model, with a median merger time near 6.5 billion years and a wide uncertainty range. ApJL
  • Simulations suggest the Sun will probably survive the merger, likely thrown much farther from the centre than today (e.g. Cox & Loeb 2008).

How the simulation works

This is a restricted three-body model in the tradition of Toomre & Toomre (1972). Each galaxy is a dark-matter halo plus bulge (Hernquist profiles: 1.5 and 1.9 trillion solar masses). The two centres follow their mutual gravity with a dynamical-friction drag, tuned so the first close pass falls at 4.0 billion years and the cores collide on the second plunge at 4.5 billion years. Up to 45,000 star, gas and dust particles then respond to both galaxies’ gravity with a leapfrog integrator in a background worker. Before 3.5 billion years the disks rotate rigidly, because tides are negligible while the galaxies are far apart.

Deliberate simplifications

  • Particles do not attract one another, so there is no disk self-gravity, gas physics or black-hole growth. Starbursts are shown as brightened gas clouds near close passes.
  • The Triangulum galaxy (M33), the Magellanic Clouds and other satellites are omitted, though they matter for the real odds.
  • Orientations of the disks and the orbit are chosen to resemble the published forecasts, not fitted to them. The Sun is one particle on a near-circular orbit; its fate here is one plausible outcome.
  • The future night sky in chapter II is seen from the Sun’s simulated position, with an imagined horizon. Brightness, colour and the distant galaxy field in chapter VII are artistic.

Facts in the captions

  • Milky Way: 100–400 billion stars, about 100,000 light-years across; the Sun is about 26,000 light-years from the centre and orbits once every ~230 million years. Andromeda holds around a trillion stars.
  • Andromeda spans about 3° of sky — six full Moons. Its light left about 2.5 million years ago, around the time our genus, Homo, appeared.
  • The Sun brightens by roughly 10% per billion years; it begins to swell toward a red giant in about 5 billion years.
  • Sand-grain scale: the Sun is 1.39 million km across and the nearest star is 4.2 light-years away, a ratio of about 29 million to one — a 0.5 mm grain and another 14 km away.
